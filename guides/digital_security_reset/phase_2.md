## Step 1: Sort out what lets someone in, or use another device

Look at the device checks first: remote access (Screen Sharing, Remote
Management, remote-access tools), browser extensions that can read sign-in
pages, a proxy or hosts-file change, and background tasks you don't recognize.
Any of these can read or redirect a new password. Until they're sorted, do
every account step below on your phone or another device you trust.

If you think a partner, ex or someone you live with is behind this, talk to an
advocate before you change account settings: signing them out or changing a
password shows them you noticed (US National Domestic Violence Hotline:
1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788).

## Step 2: Change your email password

Do this first, from a device you trust: your email is the recovery path for
almost every other account. Use a long password you haven't used anywhere else.
If you can't sign in at all, use your email provider's own account recovery
page, never a number or link from a message.

## Step 3: Sign out every device you don't recognize

In your email's security settings, open the list of devices and sessions
signed in, and sign out the ones you don't recognize. A new password does not
always sign them out.

## Step 4: Check the recovery phone and email

Remove any recovery phone number or email address that isn't yours. Add your
own first, so the account always has one.

## Step 5: Check forwarding and mail rules

Turn off forwarding you didn't set up, and delete filters or rules you didn't
make. Someone who got in can leave a rule that sends copies of your mail to
them, or hides the provider's security alerts.

## Step 6: Remove connected apps and app passwords you don't recognize

Review the apps and sites connected to your account, and any app passwords
(app-specific passwords). Remove what you don't recognize or no longer use.
App passwords can sign in without your two-step sign-in.

## Step 7: Turn on two-step sign-in for your email

If it isn't already on, turn it on in your email's security settings, and
remove any phone or authenticator on it that isn't yours.

## Step 8: Use your provider's own hacked-account page

Each provider publishes its own steps, with the exact menus:

- Google: https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825
- Microsoft: https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/help/10494
- Apple: https://support.apple.com/en-us/102560

In the desktop app, "I think I've been hacked" played as a game has the same
steps as missions ("Take your accounts back"), showing where each setting
lives at Google, Microsoft and Apple. This requires signing in to the provider
directly; the toolkit does not access account sessions or tokens, and never
asks for a password or a code.
